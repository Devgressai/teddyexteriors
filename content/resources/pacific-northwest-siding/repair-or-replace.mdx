## The short answer

Most siding problems fall into one of three buckets:

- **Cosmetic** — paint failure, surface staining, isolated impact damage. Usually fixable without replacing the siding.
- **Localized** — one wall or one elevation has damaged boards, failing caulk, or a specific flashing problem. Often fixable in place.
- **Systemic** — damage spans multiple walls, the material itself is at the end of its service life, or there's water intrusion behind the siding. Replacement is usually the right call.

A real decision depends less on the siding and more on the layers behind it. If the weather-resistive barrier (WRB) and flashing are compromised, replacing a couple of boards doesn't fix the problem — it just hides it.

## What to look for

### Signs that lean toward repair

- Damage is contained to a few boards or one wall.
- Trim and flashing are intact around windows, doors, and penetrations.
- No evidence of water staining on interior finishes near the exterior wall.
- Siding material is still available in a matching profile and finish.
- The siding is well under its typical service life for the material (fiber cement, LP SmartSide, cedar, and vinyl all have different expectations).

### Signs that lean toward replacement

- Damage shows up on multiple elevations.
- Caulk is failing across many openings — a symptom of underlying movement or expansion/contraction stress.
- Interior finishes near windows or at the base of walls show water staining or soft drywall.
- WRB or flashing is torn, missing, or improperly lapped where visible.
- The siding is at or beyond the material's typical service life.
- Mixing new siding into old would be visible even after painting.

## What a real on-site assessment includes

A fair assessment does not stop at the finish. On a repair-vs-replace visit, we are looking for:

- Fastener pull-through, delamination, or warping that indicates installation problems rather than normal weathering.
- Caulk and flashing failures at windows, doors, deck ledgers, and dissimilar-material transitions (siding to brick, siding to roof).
- Mushy or soft areas when the siding is pressed firmly — usually a sign of saturated sheathing behind.
- Interior conditions near exterior walls: staining, soft drywall, musty smell, or paint failure that aligns with the exterior.
- Evidence of pest activity in affected areas.

If the finding is "localized problem that will not recur because the WRB and flashing are intact," repair is reasonable. If the finding is "the layers behind the siding are failing," replacement is usually a better investment than repeated repairs.

## What to be skeptical of

- **A quote based on photos only.** Siding scope depends on what the walls are actually doing. A photo-only estimate for anything more than a single-board repair carries real risk of change-order surprises.
- **"We'll just slap new siding over the old."** Overcladding can be appropriate in specific cases, but it can also hide failing WRB and flashing. If a contractor is proposing to re-side without opening a representative sample to inspect the layers behind, ask why.
- **Promises of "no permit needed."** Repair scope that doesn't trip the permit threshold is common, but entire-wall replacement usually does. Jurisdictions vary. If "no permit" is central to the pitch, verify directly with the local building department.

## Making the call

A good decision balances three things:

1. **The actual damage** — scope, cause, and whether it will recur.
2. **The condition behind the siding** — WRB, flashing, and sheathing.
3. **How long you plan to own the house** — a short-horizon owner may rationally choose repair even when replacement would be a better long-term investment.

If a reasonable repair fixes the actual problem and the layers behind the siding are intact, repair makes sense. If repair is likely to be the first of several, replacement — done once, with corrected flashing — is usually the lower-regret path.
